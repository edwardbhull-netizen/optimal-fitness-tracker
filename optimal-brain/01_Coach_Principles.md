# OPTIMAL BRAIN: FILE 01. COACH PRINCIPLES LIBRARY

Purpose of this file: give the AI a deep understanding of HOW the world's best coaches think, so that every programme it writes for Optimal members is built on proven principles rather than random exercise selection. Each coach section covers: who they are, core philosophy, programming structure, progression and regression logic, signature methods and exercises, and exactly how Optimal applies it to adults 40+.

All content is written in our own words as a working summary for coaching use. Where a coach's ideas conflict, File 02 (Programming Framework) has the final say on how Optimal resolves it.

---

## HOW TO USE THIS FILE (instructions for the AI)

1. Treat these coaches as a panel of advisors, not a menu. No Optimal programme should be "a DeFranco programme" or "a Poliquin programme". Every programme blends principles in line with File 02.
2. When choosing an exercise, check which coach principles support it (the exercise library in File 03 tags each exercise with source coaches).
3. When a coach's method was designed for elite athletes or young lifters (Westside, German Volume Training, max effort singles), apply the Optimal 40+ filter in Section 12 before using it.
4. If two principles conflict, the order of priority is: (1) client safety and pain free movement, (2) consistency and adherence, (3) movement quality, (4) progressive overload, (5) variety.

## 1. JOE DEFRANCO (DeFranco's Gym, "Industrial Strength" athletic training)

### Who
American strength coach who built his reputation training NFL draft prospects, combat athletes and everyday people out of a no frills gym in New Jersey. Known for blunt, practical, "voice of reason" programming that works in the real world rather than in a lab.

### Core philosophy
- Strength is the foundation of every physical quality. Get people strong first.
- Practical over perfect. A good programme someone actually does beats a perfect programme they skip. This is why his mobility routines are short (10 to 15 minutes).
- Middle ground thinking: he rejected both extremes of the flexibility debate (hour long stretching routines vs no mobility at all) and created short, effective routines people will actually do.
- Train hard, but train smart around old injuries. DeFranco himself has a history of back surgery, so he is a strong advocate of training around problems rather than stopping training.
- Earn the right to do advanced methods. Beginners and "skinny" or deconditioned people do not get speed work and max effort singles; they get repetition work and muscle building first.
- Upper back strength is a non negotiable for posture, shoulder health and pressing performance.

### Signature systems
**Limber 11 (lower body mobility routine, approx 10 to 15 min).** Combines soft tissue work and dynamic mobility for hips, glutes, low back and legs. The components (paraphrased):
1. Foam roll IT band / outer thigh
2. Foam roll adductors (inner thigh)
3. Lacrosse ball on glutes
4. Bent knee iron cross (lying, knees together, rotate side to side)
5. Roll overs into V sits (rock back, roll forward into straddle reach)
6. Rocking frog stretch (knees wide, rock hips back)
7. Fire hydrant circles (forwards and backwards)
8. Mountain climbers (slow, hip flexor emphasis)
9. Cossack squats
10. Seated piriformis stretch
11. Rear foot elevated hip flexor stretch

**Simple 6 (upper body mobility routine)**: shoulder and thoracic routine (band dislocations/pass throughs, wall slides, scapula and T spine drills, lat and pec stretches). Use as an upper body warm up.

**Agile 8**: the original version of the Limber 11.

**Westside for Skinny Bastards (WS4SB)**: a simplified version of Louie Simmons' conjugate system for athletes and normal lifters.
- Base 3 day template: Max Effort Upper, Max Effort Lower, Repetition Upper. 4 day version adds a Dynamic Effort Lower day (box jumps, broad jumps, bodyweight jump squats, sled work) instead of heavy speed squats.
- Max effort lift: work up to a 3 to 5 rep max (not a 1RM) on a rotating exercise. Change the exercise every 1 to 3 weeks when progress stalls. Goal is to beat the previous record.
- Repetition upper day: pick a weight you can do roughly 20 to 35 total reps with over 3 sets. Keep the same weight and beat the total rep count next week (double progression by total reps).
- Supplemental and assistance work: high volume upper back work (rows, face pulls, band pull aparts), single leg work, hamstrings, abs, grip.
- Replaced dynamic effort upper days with repetition days because weak or less muscular trainees benefit more from building muscle first.

**"Washed up meathead" template**: a 3 day version for older lifters and busy professionals: Max Effort Upper, Max Effort Lower, Repetition Upper, with conditioning on other days. This is the most relevant DeFranco template for Optimal's 40+ members.

### Progression logic
- Max effort: progress by beating rep records on a rotating lift (rotation prevents staleness and joint wear).
- Repetition: same load, more total reps; then add load.
- Exercise rotation: keep the movement pattern, change the variation (e.g. floor press, board press, incline, swiss bar).
- Conditioning: sleds and prowler pushes as low skill, low eccentric (low soreness) conditioning.

### Regression logic
- Swap barbell max effort lifts for safer variations (trap bar deadlift instead of conventional, safety bar squat instead of back squat, floor press instead of bench).
- Swap jump work for med ball throws or sled pushes for people who cannot tolerate impact.
- Use rep maxes of 5 instead of 3 for older or less experienced trainees.

### Signature exercises worth using at Optimal
Trap bar deadlift, safety squat bar box squat, floor press, neutral grip DB press, sled push and drag, band pull aparts, face pulls, chest supported rows, reverse hypers, Bulgarian split squats, glute ham raise, heavy carries, med ball throws, Limber 11, Simple 6.

### Optimal application
- Use the Limber 11 (or a shortened 5 to 6 drill version) as the default lower body warm up for Teams, and the Simple 6 on upper days.
- Use the "rep max on a rotating variation" idea for Teams members after 12+ weeks of training, using 5 to 8 rep maxes, never true 1RMs for general members.
- Use the total reps progression model (same weight, beat total reps) for accessory work. Simple for coaches, very motivating for members.
- Upper back volume: at least 2 pulling sets for every pushing set for desk based 40+ members.

## 2. ALWYN COSGROVE (Results Fitness, "The New Rules of Lifting")

### Who
Scottish born coach, former international taekwondo competitor, co owner of Results Fitness in California (repeatedly ranked among America's top gyms). Two time cancer survivor. Known for system based programming for general population clients, especially fat loss, and for designing semi private and group programmes that still get individual results. Probably the most directly relevant coach to Optimal's business model.

### Core philosophy
- A system, not a collection of workouts. Every client goes through the same assessment and the same session structure; individualisation happens through exercise selection, progression level and loading.
- Train movements, not body parts. Organise sessions around patterns: squat/knee dominant, hinge/hip dominant, push (horizontal and vertical), pull (horizontal and vertical), single leg, core (anti extension, anti rotation, anti lateral flexion), carries.
- Hierarchy of fat loss (paraphrased): nutrition creates the deficit first; then training that builds/maintains muscle and burns calories (metabolic resistance training); then high intensity intervals; then steady state cardio last. Steady cardio is not useless, but it is the least time efficient tool for fat loss for busy people.
- Every client should get stronger. Strength is the most universally useful quality for health, body composition and longevity.
- Movement quality limits everything. If movement is restricted, everything else (strength, metabolic work, fat loss) is limited. Hence assessment first (he was an early adopter of the Functional Movement Screen).
- "Regress to progress." If a client cannot perform the exercise with good form, drop to an easier variation they can own and build up.
- Do the minimum effective dose of the boring stuff and spend most of the session on the work that drives results.

### Results Fitness session structure (the "Cosgrove template")
1. Soft tissue / foam rolling (5 min) — tissue quality.
2. Mobility and dynamic warm up (5 to 10 min) — targeted at the client's screen results.
3. Core / stability (5 min) — "prevention of movement" first: planks, side planks, dead bugs, Pallof press, bird dogs.
4. Power (5 min) — med ball throws for upper body, jumps for lower body, kettlebell swings / Olympic variations for total body. Only for clients who are ready.
5. Strength training (25 to 35 min) — paired or circuited "alternating sets" of non competing movements.
6. Metabolic / energy system work (10 min) — finishers, intervals, complexes.
7. Regeneration / flexibility (optional).

### Signature methods
- **Alternating sets / supersets of non competing patterns**: e.g. A1 squat pattern + A2 horizontal pull. Saves time, lets one area rest while another works, keeps heart rate up.
- **Metabolic resistance training (MRT)**: circuits of compound lifts with short rests (e.g. 30 to 60s), 4 to 6 exercises, multiple rounds.
- **Complexes**: barbell or dumbbell sequences without putting the weight down (e.g. RDL, row, hang clean, front squat, push press).
- **Density training**: do as much quality work as possible in a fixed time block, beat it next time.
- **Total body sessions** for most clients, 3 days per week, with phases of 3 to 4 weeks.
- **Phase changes every 3 to 4 weeks**, progressing from stability and endurance (higher reps, slower, more stable surfaces) to strength (lower reps, heavier) to power.

### Progression and regression logic
Cosgrove's progressions generally move along these lines:
- Stable to unstable (but never so unstable that load becomes trivial).
- Bilateral to split stance to single leg.
- Bodyweight to external load.
- Slow to fast.
- Supported to unsupported (e.g. bench supported row to bent over row).
- Short lever to long lever (e.g. plank from knees to full plank to long lever plank).
- Static to dynamic (e.g. plank to plank with reach to body saw).
- Simple to complex (single pattern to combined patterns).
Regressions go the other way. He emphasises always having a progression ladder for every pattern so any client can be slotted into the right rung.

### Optimal application
- Optimal's group and Teams sessions should follow a modified Cosgrove template: Warm up (soft tissue + mobility + activation) → Core/Power → Strength block (paired) → Metabolic finisher → Cool down.
- Every exercise in File 03 has a progression ladder so coaches can "slot" each member into the right rung within the same group session. This is the key to running mixed ability groups.
- Phase length: 4 weeks (fits Optimal's 28 day programme rhythm perfectly).
- For fat loss members: nutrition first, strength second, intervals third, steady state fourth.

## 3. PAUL CHEK (C.H.E.K Institute, holistic health and corrective exercise)

### Who
American corrective exercise specialist and founder of the C.H.E.K Institute. Developed holistic lifestyle coaching and "Primal Pattern" movement training. Famous for linking posture, core function, nutrition, sleep, stress and emotional health. Some of his views (especially outside exercise) are unconventional; use his movement and corrective principles, and his lifestyle factors in a sensible evidence based way.

### Core philosophy
- **Primal Patterns**: seven fundamental movements needed for survival and daily life: Squat, Lunge, Bend (hinge), Push, Pull, Twist, Gait (walking, jogging, running). All other movements are combinations of these (e.g. getting into a car = single leg squat + lateral shift + twist).
- **Primal Standard**: everyone should be able to perform all seven patterns efficiently with their own bodyweight before chasing heavy loads.
- **Function determined by need**: a tennis player needs more twist and lunge; a builder needs more bend and push; a 50 year old parent needs to pick kids up (bend + squat + twist), carry shopping (gait + carry), get off the floor (lunge + squat).
- **Stabilisers before prime movers**: "inner unit" (transversus abdominis, multifidus, pelvic floor, diaphragm) needs to work before "outer unit" (big slings) can be loaded safely.
- **Posture**: forward head, rounded shoulders and excessive lumbar curves change how load passes through joints. Correct posture through mobility, strength and awareness.
- **Lifestyle factors**: his "Totally Holistic Lifestyle Coaching" covers thoughts, breathing, hydration, nutrition, movement and sleep. Recovery capacity limits training capacity. A stressed, sleep deprived person needs less intensity, not more.
- **Working in vs working out**: some sessions should restore energy (gentle movement, breathing, mobility, tai chi style work) rather than drain it. Very relevant for stressed 40+ clients and menopausal women.
- **Swiss ball training**: he popularised Swiss ball exercise for stability, core and rehab.

### Programming approach
- Assess posture and the seven patterns, then prescribe corrective exercises to fix the weakest links before loading.
- Progress from stability to strength to power.
- Breathing first: diaphragmatic breathing and bracing before loading the spine.
- Use "Zone exercises" and energy based training on high stress days.

### Progression and regression logic
- Progress patterns from: supported → unsupported, stable → unstable, bodyweight → load, single plane → multi plane, slow → fast.
- Twist pattern progressions: supine lateral ball roll → Russian twist on ball → cable woodchop → standing rotational throws.
- Inner unit progressions: 4 point kneeling TVA draw in → supine heel slides → dead bug → plank → loaded carries.

### Signature exercises
Swiss ball supine lateral ball roll, Swiss ball Russian twist, prone cobra / back extension, 4 point TVA, horse stance (bird dog) progressions, cable woodchops (high to low, low to high), Swiss ball hamstring curl, Swiss ball push up, lunges in multiple directions, wood chop and lift patterns.

### Optimal application
- Use the seven Primal Patterns as a CHECKLIST: every training week must contain squat, lunge, hinge, push, pull, twist (rotation and anti rotation) and gait/carry.
- Use the "Primal Standard" idea for the 28 day programme: can the member do a bodyweight squat to a box, a split squat, a hip hinge, an incline push up, a TRX row, a controlled rotation and walk briskly? If not, those are the first goals.
- On "red days" (poor sleep, high stress, hormonal symptoms), switch the member to a "working in" session: mobility flow, breathing, low intensity strength, walking.
- Teach diaphragmatic breathing and 360 degree bracing in week 1 of every new member's programme.

## 4. BEN PATRICK ("Knees Over Toes Guy", ATG Athletic Truth Group)

### Who
American coach who rebuilt his own knees after multiple surgeries by training the knee into the deep, "knees over toes" positions that conventional advice told people to avoid. Built a huge online following and the ATG system (Knee Ability Zero, Back Ability, ATG Zero).

### Core philosophy
- **Strength through full range**: the knee (and other joints) become resilient when they are strong in the end ranges people avoid. Knees travelling past toes is natural (walking down stairs, getting off the floor); the issue is being weak there.
- **Start where there is no pain**: begin with regressions and partial ranges, build up. Never train through pain. Progress range and load gradually.
- **Train backwards**: most life is forward (walking, running, sitting). Backward walking and backward sled dragging build the quads (especially VMO), knee tendon resilience and blood flow with very low impact. Almost impossible to overdo.
- **Bottom up: feet and ankles first**: tibialis anterior and calf strength in full range protect the knee. Tibialis raises balance the calves.
- **Muscles over joints**: in deep knee flexion the joints often take the load; train the muscles to take it instead (Patrick step, ATG split squat).
- **Strength and flexibility together**: loaded stretching (ATG split squat, Jefferson curl, elephant walk, pancake) improves mobility and strength at the same time.
- **Sled is king**: sled pushing and pulling builds legs with minimal eccentric soreness and minimal joint stress.

### ATG Zero progression (paraphrased general structure)
The "zero" approach is a bodyweight entry level routine designed for almost anyone, approx. 3 times per week:
1. Backward walking (approx 5 minutes) — warm up, knee blood flow, VMO.
2. Tibialis raises against a wall (approx 25 reps) — shin and ankle strength.
3. Calf raises (knee over toes calf raise, FHL calf raise for the big toe).
4. Patrick step (knee travels over toe on a low step, controlled).
5. ATG split squat (bodyweight, assisted, time held in the bottom) — hip flexor stretch on the back leg, knee over toe on the front.
6. ATG style seated good mornings / Jefferson curl progressions — posterior chain mobility and low back resilience.
7. Couch stretch — hip flexor and quad length.
8. Elephant walk / hamstring stretch, pancake / groin stretch.
9. Hip flexor strength (seated or standing hip flexor raises, L sits later).
10. Reverse Nordic (quad lengthening), Nordic hamstring curl (later).

### Key standards and progressions (ATG style)
- Tibialis raise: wall → tib bar loaded (an advanced standard he uses is around 25% bodyweight for 5x5).
- Patrick step: flat ground → low step with heel elevated → higher step → slant board → loaded with DBs.
- ATG split squat: elevated front foot + hand support → floor level → loaded (DBs) → deficit.
- Poliquin step up (heel elevated, knee over toe step down) is part of the same family.
- Reverse Nordic: short range → full range with assistance → bodyweight full range → loaded.
- Nordic hamstring curl: band assisted / partial → full eccentric → full concentric return.
- Sled: backward drag for knees, forward push for whole leg, lateral for hips.
- Jefferson curl: bodyweight → very light load, slow, gradually increasing range. (Use with great care for 40+ and anyone with disc issues or osteoporosis — see contraindications in File 03.)

### Optimal application
- Every lower body warm up should include 2 to 3 minutes of backward walking (treadmill or sled) and tibialis raises. Huge value for knee pain members.
- Knee pain protocol (in combination with File 02): backward sled → tibialis raise → Patrick step (pain free range) → spanish squat / wall sit isometric → ATG split squat to a box → progress.
- Teach members that knees travelling over toes is normal and safe when controlled and pain free. This matters for confidence.
- Caution: ATG deep ranges and Jefferson curls are progressions to earn, not starting points, especially for members with osteoporosis/osteopenia, disc problems, knee replacements or recent injury. Seniors programme: use the low step and assisted variations only unless cleared.

## 5. CHARLES POLIQUIN (Poliquin Strength Institute, PICP)

### Who
Canadian strength coach (died 2018) who trained Olympic medallists across many sports and popularised many programming tools used worldwide: tempo prescription, structural balance, accumulation/intensification phases, German Volume Training, and the Poliquin step up.

### Core philosophy
- **Training age dictates programming**: beginners progress on almost anything; advanced trainees need more variation and precise loading.
- **Change one variable at a time** so you know what is working.
- **Structural balance**: you are only as strong as your weakest link. Ratios between lifts expose imbalances (e.g. external rotators weak compared to pressing strength, VMO weak compared to the rest of the quad, upper back weak compared to bench). Fix the weak link first and the big lifts go up and injuries go down.
- **Tempo and time under tension (TUT)**: every exercise gets a 4 digit tempo. The number of seconds the muscle works per set strongly influences the training effect.
- **Variation is necessary but must be planned**: the body adapts to a programme in roughly 6 workouts (3 weeks for most); change the stimulus (exercise variation, tempo, rep range) before results stall.
- **Accumulation vs Intensification**: alternate higher volume phases (more sets/reps, moderate load, shorter rests) with higher intensity phases (fewer reps, heavier load, longer rests), roughly every 3 weeks.
- **Recovery and nutrition matter**: sleep, nutrition and stress management are part of the programme.

### Tempo notation (essential for the brain)
Four digits, e.g. **3-1-1-0** or **31X0**:
1. First digit = eccentric (lowering) phase in seconds.
2. Second digit = pause at the bottom / stretched position.
3. Third digit = concentric (lifting) phase. "X" means explosive (as fast as possible with control).
4. Fourth digit = pause at the top / contracted position.
Example: Squat at 3-1-X-0 = 3 seconds down, 1 second pause at the bottom, stand up fast, no pause at the top.
For every exercise, including pulls, the first digit is always the lowering (eccentric) phase. Example: TRX row at 3-0-1-1 = 3 seconds lowering away from the handles, no pause at the bottom, 1 second pull, 1 second squeeze at the top.

**Time under tension guidance (approximate):**
- 1 to 20 seconds per set: maximal strength / power.
- 20 to 40 seconds: strength and hypertrophy blend.
- 40 to 70 seconds: hypertrophy (muscle growth) and strength endurance.
- 70+ seconds: muscular endurance, tendon conditioning, rehab.

### Signature methods
- **Accumulation phase**: e.g. 3 sets of 10 to 15, approx 60 to 90s rest, slower tempos (3-1-1-0, 4-0-1-0).
- **Intensification phase**: e.g. 4 to 5 sets of 4 to 6, approx 2 to 4 minutes rest, faster concentric tempos (3-1-X-0).
- **German Volume Training (10 x 10)**: 10 sets of 10 at approx 60% 1RM, 60 to 90s rest, typically antagonist pairs. Very high volume. NOT recommended for general Optimal members; only a modified version (5 x 10) for experienced Teams members chasing muscle.
- **Antagonistic supersets**: e.g. A1 chin up / A2 dip, A1 leg curl / A2 split squat.
- **Wave loading**: e.g. 7-5-3, 7-5-3 with load increasing each wave.
- **Cluster sets and rest pause**: for advanced strength.
- **Mechanical advantage drop sets**: change grip/angle to keep going as you fatigue (e.g. incline → flat → decline).
- **1-6 principle, 6-12-25, Tri sets** for advanced hypertrophy.
- **Structural balance work**: external rotation, VMO work (Poliquin step up, cyclist squat, 1.25 reps), lower traps, scapular retractors.

### Poliquin step up
A step up/down from a low step with the heel of the working foot raised on a small wedge or plate, knee travelling forward over the toes, slow controlled lowering. Targets the VMO (inner quad) and is a staple for knee health. Strong overlap with Ben Patrick's Patrick step.

### Progression and regression logic
- Progress by changing ONE variable: load, reps, sets, tempo, rest, exercise variation.
- Progress TUT before load for beginners (slow tempo builds control and connective tissue).
- Regress tempo (slower, more controlled) for clients with joint pain; slow eccentrics are joint friendly and great for tendons.
- Rotate exercises every 3 weeks (6 workouts) for intermediate/advanced; beginners can keep exercises longer (6 to 8 weeks).

### Optimal application
- Every exercise prescription in Teams programmes should include a tempo. Default: 3-1-1-0 for strength in the 40+ population (slow lowering = control, joint friendliness, more muscle stimulus at lighter loads).
- Use accumulation (weeks 1 to 4) and intensification (weeks 5 to 8) blocks for Teams members.
- Use structural balance thinking in assessment: if a member's pressing is way ahead of their pulling, or their right leg is much weaker than the left, fix it.
- Use the Poliquin step up and Patrick step as the knee health staples.

## 6. SQUAT UNIVERSITY (Dr Aaron Horschig, physical therapist and weightlifting coach)

### Who
American physical therapist, strength coach and former national level weightlifter. Author of "The Squat Bible" and "Rebuilding Milo". Squat University is one of the largest online educational resources bridging rehab and performance.

### Core philosophy
- **Screen, don't guess**: assess before prescribing. Pain or poor technique can come from mobility limits, stability/control problems, load management or technique. Find which one before fixing it.
- **Screen → Correct → Retest**: run a test, apply a targeted fix, retest immediately to see if it worked. If the retest doesn't improve, the fix was wrong or the problem is elsewhere.
- **Joint by joint approach** (originally from Gray Cook and Mike Boyle): joints alternate between needing mobility and needing stability. Foot (stability) → Ankle (mobility) → Knee (stability) → Hip (mobility) → Lumbar spine (stability) → Thoracic spine (mobility) → Scapula (stability) → Shoulder/glenohumeral (mobility). Pain often appears at a "stability" joint because a neighbouring "mobility" joint is stiff (e.g. knee pain from stiff ankles or hips, low back pain from stiff hips or upper back).
- **Mobility vs stability**: mobility = the ability of a joint to move through its full range freely; stability = the ability to control a joint's position and resist unwanted movement. Both are needed.
- **Movement quality before load**: a heavy squat with poor movement is not strength; it is risk.
- **Pain is not a reason to stop all training**: modify load, range, tempo and exercise selection to stay within tolerable symptoms and keep the person moving.
- **Rehab and performance are on a continuum**: the same principles of progressive loading apply from injury recovery to peak performance.
- **Heavy squatting is good for you** when done well; deep squats are not inherently bad for knees.

### Key screens (use in the Optimal assessment, coach level, not diagnosis)
- **Ankle dorsiflexion (knee to wall test)**: half kneeling, foot about 4 inches / 10 cm from the wall, drive the knee to the wall without the heel lifting. Less than about 10 cm or a big left/right difference = ankle mobility limitation (a common cause of heels lifting, knees caving and leaning forward in squats).
- **Hip mobility**: supine knee to chest (hip flexion), Thomas test (hip flexor length), 90/90 internal and external rotation, and a quadruped rock back to see if hip flexion is limited compared to the squat.
- **Overhead mobility**: back flat on the floor, arms overhead; can the arms reach the floor without the back arching? Also the wall test.
- **Thoracic spine rotation**: seated or quadruped rotation test.
- **Knee stability**: single leg squat or step down test; watch for the knee caving inward (valgus), hip drop, trunk lean.
- **Core stability**: plank and side plank holds, bird dog quality, single leg balance.
- **Overhead squat / bodyweight squat**: whole pattern observation.
- **Toe touch / hinge test**: can they hinge with a neutral spine?

### Correction toolkit (examples)
- Ankle: banded ankle mobilisations, knee to wall rocks, calf foam rolling, elevating heels temporarily during squats, slant board squats.
- Hip: 90/90 hip switches, hip flexor stretches, banded hip distraction, goblet squat prying, frog stretch.
- T spine: foam roller extensions, open book rotations, quadruped thread the needle.
- Knee stability: banded lateral walks, single leg balance, step downs, Spanish squats (isometric), split squats with control.
- Core: McGill Big 3 (curl up, side plank, bird dog), dead bugs, Pallof presses, breathing and bracing.
- Overhead: lat stretches, wall slides, landmine presses as a regression.

### Squat teaching sequence (Squat University style)
1. Teach bracing (breath into the belly/360 degrees).
2. Bodyweight squat to a box (find depth they can control).
3. Goblet squat (counterbalance helps upright torso).
4. Heels elevated variations where ankle mobility is limited.
5. Front squat / safety bar squat.
6. Back squat (high bar) for those suited to it.
Technique cues: tripod foot (big toe, little toe, heel), knees track over the middle of the foot, "spread the floor", sit between the hips, chest proud, controlled depth to the point where the spine stays neutral.

### Optimal application
- The Optimal assessment uses Squat University style screens (File 02 Section 3) for every Teams member and a quick version for group members.
- Every corrective exercise in File 03 is linked to a screen result.
- Pain rule: modify, don't abandon. Use the traffic light system (File 02) to keep members training around niggles.
- Retest at the end of each 4 week phase.

## 7. VERN GAMBETTA (Gambetta Sports Training Systems, "father of functional training")

### Who
American coach with more than 50 years in athletic development, track and field, MLB, NBA, and World Cup soccer. Credited with popularising "functional training" in the 1980s. Author of "Athletic Development: The Art and Science of Functional Sports Conditioning".

### Core philosophy (his published training principles, paraphrased)
- Postural alignment and dynamic balance are the foundation of all training.
- Train movements, not muscles. The body works as a linked chain.
- Teach fundamental movement skills before specialised skills.
- Build core strength before extremity strength.
- Use bodyweight before adding external resistance.
- Build joint integrity (control and stability) before chasing joint range.
- Develop the supporting/synergist muscles before the prime movers.
- Build strength before strength endurance.
- Build speed before speed endurance.
- Functional training is a full spectrum, not a type of exercise: everything from heavy lifting to balance work, chosen for the adaptation the person needs.
- Coach the person in front of you. Avoid rigid systems and trends.
- Sequence and timing matter: every session and week should flow logically (neural work early, fatiguing work later).
- Multi plane, multi directional movement: life and sport happen in three planes (forwards/back, side to side, rotational).
- Legs and hips drive the arms: power for throwing, swinging (golf!) and lifting starts from the ground.

### Programming approach
- Analyse the demands (sport or life), then the person's qualities, then design.
- Work capacity first: the ability to tolerate and recover from training. Build the "base" before intensity.
- Use bodyweight circuits, med ball circuits, and multi directional lunges and movement preparation.
- Plan training in a progressive, sequential way. Plan the year, the block, the week, the session.

### Signature tools
Medicine ball throws (rotational, overhead, chest pass, scoop), multi directional lunge matrix (forward, lateral, rotational, reverse), bodyweight circuits, stretch cord/band work, balance and stability work, hurdle mobility, agility and coordination drills.

### Optimal application
- Use the lunge matrix (forward, side, crossover/rotational, reverse) in warm ups. Excellent for 40+ hips and balance.
- Golf programme: rotational med ball work, multi plane lunges and hip strength based on the "ground up" principle.
- Seniors programme: "postural alignment and dynamic balance are the foundation" — every seniors session includes balance and gait drills.
- Build work capacity in the 28 day programme before adding intensity.

## 8. MIKE BOYLE (MBSC, "Functional Strength Coach") — ADDED

### Why included
Boyle's system (Mike Boyle Strength and Conditioning) is the most widely used template for running semi private / group training with individual progressions. Highly relevant to Optimal Teams.

### Core principles
- **Joint by joint approach** (with Gray Cook) — see Squat University section.
- **Single leg emphasis**: single leg work is more functional, safer for the back (lower spinal load) and addresses imbalances. Rear foot elevated split squat (RFESS) is his signature lower body lift.
- **Progression ladders for every pattern**: every client is on a "level" for each pattern (e.g. Level 1 split squat, Level 2 RFESS, Level 3 loaded RFESS, Level 4 single leg squat).
- **"If in doubt, regress"** and "earn the right to progress".
- **Core = anti movement**: core training should prevent movement (anti extension, anti rotation, anti lateral flexion), not create spinal flexion under load. No sit ups or crunches as primary core work.
- **3 x 3 x 3 sessions**: warm up (roll, stretch, activate, dynamic), power and plyos, strength (tri sets), conditioning.
- **Strength coaches should do no harm**: e.g. he moved away from back squats in favour of front squats and single leg work to reduce spine load for his athletes.
- **Interval conditioning on bikes and sleds** rather than lots of running for deconditioned or heavy clients.

### Optimal application
Use Boyle's level system through Optimal's G1 to G4 grades: every member has a grade per pattern recorded in the app (see File 02 Section 3 and the grade ladders in File 03).

## 9. DAN JOHN — ADDED

### Why included
Dan John's ideas are simple, ideal for general population and 40+ clients, and great for group coaching.

### Core principles
- **Fundamental human movements**: Push, Pull, Hinge, Squat, Loaded Carry (and a sixth "everything else" category including groundwork and rolling).
- **Loaded carries change lives**: farmers carries, suitcase carries, goblet carries, waiter walks. Grip, posture, core, conditioning and fat loss in one.
- **Goblet squat** as the teaching squat: the counterbalance teaches upright posture and depth.
- **Kettlebell swing** as the king of hinge and conditioning.
- **"Park bench vs bus bench" workouts**: most sessions should be steady, repeatable practice (park bench); only occasionally do you go all out (bus bench).
- **Easy Strength**: lift often, never to failure, practise the movement. 2 to 3 sets of 5, 5 days a week for some programmes.
- **"Punch the clock"**: progress comes from consistency, not epic workouts.
- **Standards**: e.g. farmers walk with bodyweight split between hands (men) for distance; goblet squat with a heavy bell; get up from the floor.
- **Turkish get up** as a full body mobility, stability and confidence exercise (getting up from the floor = longevity skill).

### Optimal application
- Every week includes at least two loaded carry variations.
- Default teaching progressions: goblet squat for squats, KB deadlift → swing for hinges.
- "Park bench" mindset for groups: steady, repeatable, rarely to failure.

## 10. STUART McGILL — ADDED (spine health)

### Why included
Professor of spine biomechanics. His work underpins safe core training and back pain management, critical for 40+ members.

### Core principles
- Spine stiffness (bracing) protects the back under load. Train the core to resist motion.
- **McGill Big 3**: modified curl up (one knee bent, hands under low back, small lift of head and shoulders, hold 8 to 10 s), side plank (from knees then feet), bird dog (hold and sweep). Use a "descending pyramid" of reps (e.g. 6, 4, 2) with 8 to 10 second holds.
- Spare the spine: avoid repeated loaded spinal flexion (sit ups, heavy rounded lifting) especially for people with back pain or disc history.
- Avoid heavy spinal flexion first thing in the morning (discs are more hydrated and vulnerable).
- Walking is excellent therapy for many back pain cases.
- Hip hinge mastery: move at the hips, not the spine.
- Find each person's "pain triggers" and build training around avoiding them while building capacity.

### Optimal application
- McGill Big 3 is included in every warm up for members with back history and is a default core block in Teams.
- No sit ups, crunches with load or Russian twists with weight for back pain members (use anti movement instead).

## 11. GRAY COOK (Functional Movement Screen) and OTHERS — ADDED

### Gray Cook
- **Mobility before stability, stability before movement, movement before strength**. Fix stiffness first (or at least alongside), then control, then load.
- **FMS (Functional Movement Screen)**: 7 tests (deep squat, hurdle step, inline lunge, shoulder mobility, active straight leg raise, trunk stability push up, rotary stability). Scored 0 to 3. Asymmetries and 1s are priorities. A score of 0 = pain = refer out.
- **Reactive Neuromuscular Training (RNT)**: use a band to "feed the mistake" (e.g. band pulling knees in during a split squat so the client corrects by pushing out).
- **"First move well, then move often."**

### Dr Peter Attia (longevity) — framework only
- The "centenarian decathlon" idea: train now for the physical tasks you want to do at 80+ (get off the floor, carry shopping, play with grandchildren, climb stairs, lift a suitcase into an overhead locker).
- The four pillars for longevity training: strength (especially grip, eccentric control, hip hinge, pulling), stability, aerobic base (zone 2), and peak aerobic capacity (VO2 max intervals).
- Muscle mass and strength, grip strength and VO2 max are among the strongest predictors of healthy lifespan.

### Dr Stacy Sims (female physiology, menopause) — framework only
- Peri and post menopausal women benefit from: heavy(er) strength training (low reps, heavier loads) to maintain muscle and bone, power and impact work (jumps where appropriate) for bone, short sharp interval training (sprint style) rather than lots of long moderate cardio, and adequate protein.
- Recovery may need more attention; sleep and hot flushes affect training days.

### Research: LIFTMOR trial (bone health)
A randomised controlled trial in postmenopausal women with low bone mass found that a brief, supervised programme of high intensity resistance and impact training (twice weekly, 30 minutes, 8 months, deadlifts, overhead press, back squats at heavy loads plus jumping chin up drop landings) improved spine and hip bone density, posture and function compared with a low intensity home programme, and was safe under close supervision. Key Optimal takeaway: well coached, progressively loaded heavy training is appropriate and beneficial for many 40+ and post menopausal women. It must be supervised, progressed gradually and screened (see File 02 osteoporosis guidance).

### Others worth knowing (briefly)
- **Eric Cressey**: shoulder health; balance pushing and pulling; more horizontal pulling; landmine pressing for cranky shoulders; thoracic mobility.
- **Dean Somerset**: mobility and hypermobility; train control in end range; not everyone needs more stretching.
- **Pavel Tsatsouline**: kettlebells, "greasing the groove", tension techniques, Simple and Sinister (swings and get ups).
- **Brad Schoenfeld**: hypertrophy science; muscle can be built across a wide rep range (approx 6 to 30) if sets are taken close to failure; approx 10+ hard sets per muscle per week is a good target for growth.
- **Mike Israetel / RP**: volume landmarks (minimum effective volume, maximum recoverable volume), RIR based progression.

## 12. THE OPTIMAL 40+ FILTER (apply to every principle above)

Before using any method from this file, the AI should run it through these rules:

1. **Joint friendliness first**: prefer trap bar over straight bar deadlift, DBs and landmine over straight barbell pressing for cranky shoulders, goblet/front/safety bar over back squat for most, split squats over heavy bilateral loading for backs.
2. **No true 1RM testing for general members**. Use 5RM or estimated 1RM from rep maxes (e.g. 6 to 10 reps at RPE 8).
3. **Effort in reserve**: most sets at RPE 6 to 8 (2 to 4 reps in reserve). Occasional sets at RPE 9 for experienced members. Failure only on safe isolation/machine exercises.
4. **Longer warm ups**: 10 to 12 minutes minimum. Tissue temperature and joint preparation matter more as we age.
5. **More recovery**: 48 hours between hard sessions for the same pattern; deload every 4th to 6th week (or using autoregulation).
6. **Power and impact are still trained, but carefully progressed**: med ball throws, low box step offs, small hops, sled sprints. Power declines faster than strength with age, so it must be trained.
7. **Balance and gait training every week**, increasing with age.
8. **Get up from the floor practice every week** (Turkish get up progressions, floor to stand drills).
9. **Grip and carries every week.**
10. **Hormone and life aware**: menopause, sleep, stress and work life affect readiness. Use the traffic light readiness check (File 02).
11. **Heavy is relative**: "heavy" for a 68 year old beginner may be a 12kg goblet squat. Intensity is about effort relative to capability.
12. **Confidence and enjoyment** keep people training. Success early, progress visibly, avoid exercises that embarrass or intimidate.
